The shop was ten minutes away if you followed the road. Ellen almost never followed the road home.

There was a path behind the last houses, past a fence that leaned toward the sea. In summer it smelled of warm grass. In winter she walked with her shoulders up and her shopping bag tucked under her coat, as though the potatoes needed shelter too.

Her grandson once asked why they always went that way. They had ice cream, after all, and ice cream was an argument for speed.

“We have time,” she said. “Take the long way.”

They stopped to look at a boat being painted, then at a stone with a pale line running through it. Ellen knew the difference between stopping to look and stopping to explain. She let him turn the stone over without giving it a lesson.

By the time they reached her kitchen, the ice cream was soft at the edges. She divided it into bowls and called that an improvement.

Years later, he could not have drawn the exact route. He remembered the rasp of the shopping bag against her coat. He remembered that she sometimes fell quiet, and that the quiet did not need fixing. He remembered arriving home with something small in his pocket.

Ellen kept a dish of those stones on the windowsill. When someone asked which beach they came from, she would pick one up, examine it, and give the honest answer: “One of our walks.”
