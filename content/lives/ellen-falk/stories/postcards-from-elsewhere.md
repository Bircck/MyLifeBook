Ellen’s postcards rarely described the view printed on the front.

From a city full of celebrated buildings, she wrote about a woman selling pears from a wooden cart. From a seaside guesthouse, she reported that the breakfast rolls were excellent and the wardrobe made a noise like a small boat.

She liked travelling. She also liked coming home and discovering that the house still smelled like her house. Neither pleasure seemed to diminish the other.

On one journey she and her friend Liv missed their stop. They noticed only when the other passengers began putting on coats and collecting bags. Liv worried about the timetable. Ellen worried about where they might get lunch.

They found a café with three tables and ordered by pointing. The soup contained something green they could not name. They liked it enough to ask, then failed entirely to understand the answer.

The postcard from that afternoon said: “We have been somewhere we did not intend to go. Very good soup.”

Her daughter kept it on the fridge until the corners curled. It was a useful message to encounter while searching for the milk.

Ellen did not collect countries or count journeys. Her evidence of having been away fitted into a kitchen drawer: ticket stubs, a paper napkin with an address, a photograph of Liv laughing at something outside the frame.

On the back of that photograph, Ellen had written only the year and “before we found the soup.” It was enough to bring the whole afternoon back.
