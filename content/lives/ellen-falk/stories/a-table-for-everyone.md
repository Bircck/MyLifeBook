The kitchen table had one short leg. Ellen solved this with a folded envelope, replaced whenever somebody swept too enthusiastically.

It seated four properly and seven with goodwill. At seven, someone had to sit by the drawer and agree not to need anything from it. The smallest child got the stool from the back door, which made the meal feel like a promotion.

Nobody remembers Ellen using the word *hosting*. People came over. She put the kettle on. If it was near supper, she opened the saucepan and considered the situation.

An extra potato was often the answer. Sometimes it was bread cut a little thinner, or a fried egg on top. There was no performance of abundance. She was simply good at making a meal stretch without making a visitor feel responsible for the stretching.

One wet Thursday, a neighbour arrived to return a ladder and stayed until the washing-up. He had been living alone for a few months. They talked about the ladder, then the rain, then nothing much at all.

Before he left, Ellen wrapped two slices of cake in the paper bag the flour had come in. “For tomorrow,” she said, giving the gift a practical purpose so he would not have to thank her too much.

The table passed to her daughter. The envelope passed with it. A proper repair has been discussed more than once, usually over dinner, when there are too many plates on the table to turn it over.
