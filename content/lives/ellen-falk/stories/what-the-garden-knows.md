Ellen made a garden plan on the back of a large envelope each February. Straight lines for the beds. Circles for the pots. A small, optimistic space labelled “peas”.

By June, the nasturtiums had crossed a path, the peas were negotiating with the fence, and something she had not planted was doing exceptionally well by the shed.

She sometimes moved the volunteer plants. Sometimes she moved the plan.

The garden was small enough to hear the kettle from the far end. There were vegetables, flowers for cutting, and a chair positioned where the evening light reached last. Ellen said the chair was necessary equipment. A visitor who offered to help was eventually made to test it.

In her last summer, she did less digging and more directing. Her instructions were precise about watering and surprisingly relaxed about weeds. A granddaughter pulled up what she thought was an intruder and held it out for inspection.

“I was rather waiting to see what that would do,” Ellen said.

Then they both laughed, and the plant went into a pot for a second chance.

The following spring, her daughter found the old envelopes in the shed. Some had shopping lists on the other side. One had a phone number without a name. None was a complete guide to keeping the garden.

She planted peas, badly spaced, and put the chair back in its usual place. When something unfamiliar appeared near the step, she left it alone for a while.
