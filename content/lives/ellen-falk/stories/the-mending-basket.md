There was always something waiting in the basket beside Ellen’s armchair: a cuff coming loose, a pocket with an escape route, a soft toy whose adventures had become visible.

She kept buttons in a biscuit tin. Opening it produced a smell of metal and old cloth, followed by the promise of finding exactly the right thing. Usually they found something nearly right. Ellen considered nearly right a perfectly reasonable place to begin.

When her granddaughter brought a torn blue shirt, Ellen did not take it away and return it repaired. She made room beside her, threaded a second needle, and gave her a scrap to practise on.

The first stitches were enormous. The next few were too tight. Ellen loosened one with the tip of her needle, quietly, as though they were both investigating a problem with the fabric.

“Leave it a little room,” she said. “It has to move with you.”

The patch was yellow because that was the piece they liked. It remained visible. The shirt went through another two summers, and the patch became the part its owner loved best.

Ellen did throw things away. The kettle that tripped the electricity did not become a lesson in endurance. But she disliked the moment when an object was declared finished before anyone had looked closely at it.

After she died, the family divided the useful things first. The button tin took longer. Nobody needed that many buttons. Several people needed to open it once more.
