Milo noticed the moon from the back seat on the way home. It appeared in one window, vanished behind a building, then returned in another.

“Is it coming with us?”

His dad explained about distance. Milo listened, then asked whether the moon could see their car. This required a different explanation, and by then they had arrived.

At bedtime, Milo began a list. Could you hear your own footsteps there? Would a sandwich taste the same? If you took a balloon, what would happen? His dad knew some answers, thought he knew others, and wrote a small question mark beside the rest.

The list continued onto the back of the paper. There was a practical section about toilets and a complicated section about birthdays. Milo was particularly concerned about whether astronauts remembered to bring birthday candles.

They chose three questions to look up together the next day. Milo wanted to choose all of them. A compromise was reached: three questions, with permission to add more.

Before the light went off, he asked for the curtain to stay open a little. The moon was no longer visible from his window.

“It’ll be somewhere,” he said.

The list is still beside his bed. It now includes a question about worms, because a notebook does not have to stay on one subject.
